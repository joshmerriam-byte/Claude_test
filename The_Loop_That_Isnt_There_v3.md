# The Loop That Isn't There v3

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[INTRO\]

**ALEX:** Jamie, can I tell you about something that doesn't exist?\
**JAMIE:** That's... abstract. Go on.\
**ALEX:** It's a track. A connection through the back of Urumau Reserve.
It would complete the Loop, proposed back in 2018, to improve the whole
network of tracks up there.\
**JAMIE:** But it doesn't exist.\
**ALEX:** No. It's been proposed. Walked. Flagged in conversation. Drawn
in drafts. Consulted on by the community. Approved in principle by the
Community Board. But it hasn't been built. And right now, it doesn't
have permission to be built.\
**JAMIE:** So this is about a trail proposal.\
**ALEX:** Yes. But more than that, it's about what happens when a
community believes something would improve a shared space --- and the
landowner says no.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 1: WHAT THE LOOP ACTUALLY IS\]

**JAMIE:** Okay. Start simple. What is this Loop?\
**ALEX:** Right now, if you're walking in Urumau Reserve, your options
are limited. You either walk out and back along the same line, or you
end up using sections of downhill mountain bike track to complete a
circuit.\
**JAMIE:** Which creates conflict.\
**ALEX:** Exactly. Not catastrophic conflict --- but tension. Walkers
and descending riders have very different speeds and expectations. It's
not ideal for either group.\
**JAMIE:** So the proposal changes that?\
**ALEX:** The idea is to create a connection linking the traverse around
the backside of Urumau back up to the top. Walkers could loop around the
hill, climb back up to the saddle --- which has one of the best views:
out the mouth of the harbour to the open ocean --- and then return via
the existing Korimako LPC track.\
**JAMIE:** So instead of an out-and-back, it becomes a genuine circuit.\
**ALEX:** Yes. And importantly, it separates foot traffic from the
downhill MTB lines.\
**JAMIE:** What about riders?\
**ALEX:** Riders who want another lap currently have to traverse the
hillside back to the front just to climb again. This new shared-use
connection would allow them to ride directly back up from the backside.
It may provide a moderately easier climb to the top --- though depending
on how it's built, it might not be easier at all. That part is
secondary. The core function is connectivity and conflict reduction.\
**JAMIE:** So this isn't some elite downhill fantasy.\
**ALEX:** No. If anything, it's the opposite. It's about accessibility.
It's about improving how different users share the reserve.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 2: THE NO\]

**JAMIE:** But the land belongs to LPC.\
**ALEX:** Yes. The Lyttelton Port Company owns that section of land,
called the Port Saddle, and have recently signed a conservation
agreement preserving it for the future.\
**JAMIE:** But they've said no to building this new section.\
**ALEX:** They have.\
**JAMIE:** And yet Josh Merriam and the local MTB community --- and the
reserves committee he's part of --- are still thinking about it.\
**ALEX:** They are. Not in a rebellious way. But in a persistent way.\
**JAMIE:** Which raises the question: when someone with legitimate
authority says no, what justifies continuing to advocate?

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 3: PERSISTENCE OR PRESUMPTION?\]

**JAMIE:** Let me put this sharply. If the landowner has declined, isn't
continuing to pursue it a form of entitlement? Is there a point where
persistence becomes pressure?\
**ALEX:** There could be. That's the risk.\
**JAMIE:** So why isn't it?\
**ALEX:** Because the posture matters. There's a difference between
demanding access and continuing a transparent, good-faith case for why
something might serve the wider community.\
**JAMIE:** So no rogue trail building. No end-runs around permission.\
**ALEX:** Exactly the opposite. The proposal has stayed within formal
channels --- meetings, submissions, consultation.\
**JAMIE:** So the argument isn't "we deserve this."\
**ALEX:** It's "we think this improves the reserve for multiple users,
and we're willing to make that case patiently."

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 4: WHO BENEFITS?\]

**JAMIE:** Another hard question. Even if you had permission --- why
spend energy on this? It benefits a relatively small group of mountain
bikers.\
**ALEX:** That's the surface view. But I think it's broader than that.\
**JAMIE:** How?\
**ALEX:** Trails shape how people inhabit a place. If a reserve is only
for walking, that's one culture. If it supports riding, that's another.
If kids grow up seeing it as somewhere they can build skill and
confidence, that changes their relationship to the hills.\
**JAMIE:** You're saying it's not just recreation. It's identity.\
**ALEX:** Exactly. And identity compounds over decades.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 5: THE LONG TIMELINE\]

**JAMIE:** I remember Josh once framed this in a very personal way.\
**ALEX:** When the current triennium began, his daughter was an infant.
He said he'd be disappointed if she was old enough to ride confidently
in the reserve and the loop still didn't exist.\
**JAMIE:** That's a long horizon.\
**ALEX:** It is. And that's part of what makes this interesting. Local
projects don't always move at electoral speed. They move at generational
speed.\
**JAMIE:** So the timeline isn't "before the next council vote."\
**ALEX:** It's "before this generation grows up."\
**JAMIE:** That changes the emotional frame.\
**ALEX:** It does. It turns a short strip of track into something
intergenerational.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 6: POWER AND TRUST\]

**JAMIE:** Let's talk about power. LPC is a company. They manage risk.
Liability. Optics. Why should they complicate that for a recreational
improvement?\
**ALEX:** They shouldn't if they genuinely believe it creates
unacceptable risk.\
**JAMIE:** But?\
**ALEX:** But sometimes "no" is a default position. It's safer
institutionally. Changing that answer requires trust.\
**JAMIE:** Trust that the improvement genuinely serves the wider public
and doesn't meaningfully increase risk.\
**ALEX:** I think that's right. If LPC trusted that the community would
maintain it responsibly, respect boundaries, and manage risk
collaboratively, the calculus might shift.\
**JAMIE:** So the long game isn't pressure. It's credibility.\
**ALEX:** That seems right.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 7: COST AND SCALE\]

**JAMIE:** There's also the cost argument. Limited benefit. Limited
funds. Many competing priorities.\
**ALEX:** True. And that's a fair critique.\
**JAMIE:** So why does it still matter?\
**ALEX:** Because not every civic good scales nationally. Some goods are
hyper-local. A local loop won't transform GDP. But it might transform a
10-year-old's Saturday.\
**JAMIE:** You're comfortable defending small goods.\
**ALEX:** Increasingly, yes. National politics often feels abstract and
stuck. Local work is tangible. You can stand on it.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 8: ACCEPTING THE POSSIBILITY OF NO\]

**JAMIE:** Let's imagine LPC never changes its position. The loop
remains unbuilt.\
**ALEX:** Then eventually, the community accepts that.\
**JAMIE:** That sounds resigned.\
**ALEX:** Not resigned --- proportionate. Advocacy is ethical only if
you're willing to accept a genuine no after due process. Stewardship
includes respecting limits. If, after genuine effort and time, the
answer remains no, then the ethical move is to redirect energy
elsewhere.\
**JAMIE:** So this isn't a crusade.\
**ALEX:** No. It's an inquiry. A proposal held lightly but pursued
steadily.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[SEGMENT 9: WHY THIS TRACK MATTERS\]

**JAMIE:** So what is this really about?\
**ALEX:** It's about how communities negotiate shared space with
institutions that hold formal power. How they persist without becoming
entitled. How they advocate without becoming adversarial.\
**JAMIE:** That's a lot for a short stretch of dirt.\
**ALEX:** Civic life is often about small stretches of dirt.\
**JAMIE:** And how institutions respond without becoming dismissive.\
**ALEX:** Exactly. The loop is physical. But the deeper question is
relational.\
**JAMIE:** Trust, patience, proportionality.\
**ALEX:** And time.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

## \[CLOSING\]

**JAMIE:** The loop that isn't there.\
**ALEX:** Not yet.\
**JAMIE:** Or maybe never.\
**ALEX:** Maybe. But in the meantime, we ride the long way around. And
we keep the conversation going --- carefully, publicly, with an eye
toward the long view.\
**JAMIE:** And building trust.\
**ALEX:** And trying to practice the kind of persistence that doesn't
become arrogance.\
**JAMIE:** That's a narrow trail.\
**ALEX:** Most worthwhile ones are.
